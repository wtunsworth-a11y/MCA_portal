Rationale & purpose · Oro (Northern) Province, PNG

The Oro Data Portal

Putting conservation and land-use data in the hands of provincial decision-makers — and why it fills a real gap.

Oro (Northern) Province, Papua New Guinea · Managalas & Oro Province Component, EU-FCCB Nexus Programme · 2026

In one paragraph

Decisions about Oro Province's forests, farms and conservation areas are being made with very little current, accessible data. The information that does exist — satellite monitoring, land-cover maps, historical records — is scattered, technical, and out of reach for the officers, landowners and cooperatives who need it. The Oro Data Portal closes that gap: it brings free satellite data and the region's own records together in one place, keeps them current automatically, and presents them so that a non-specialist can find an answer in minutes. It turns data that already exists but goes unused into a practical basis for provincial decisions.

1. The problem: deciding without data

Papua New Guinea has a well-documented data deficit, and it is sharpest exactly where decisions about land and forests are made — at the provincial and local level.

The national picture is stark. The country's own Strategy for the Development of Statistics identifies data gaps, low-quality data and poor data access as core weaknesses of the statistical system.[1] PNG's 2025 Voluntary National Review to the United Nations could measure only 87 of 169 Sustainable Development Goal targets — the rest could not be assessed for want of data.[2] Even the population is uncertain: modelled estimates have put it near 17 million against an official 9.4 million, a gap with direct consequences for planning and resource allocation.[3]

At the sub-national level the problem compounds. Analysts have found that lower-level development plans in PNG are unlikely to rest on sound data, and that the data underlying existing plans has become outdated.[4] Where provinces have piloted geospatial tools — mapping roads, schools and health posts with satellite imagery and GPS — the result has been markedly better local planning, which shows both the demand and the feasibility.[5] But these efforts are isolated; there is no standing, current, province-wide picture that a decision-maker can simply open.

Oro Province feels this acutely because so much is at stake on its land. Oil palm is the province's principal industry — Oro is the only province in PNG where it is the leading primary crop — grown by estates such as Higaturu alongside thousands of smallholder blocks; cocoa is grown by nearly every household.[6] These are expanding, forest-adjacent land uses. At the same time Oro holds the Managalas Conservation Area, at roughly 360,000 hectares the largest conservation area in the country and the only large protected area gazetted in PNG since 2010.[7] Weighing agricultural expansion, livelihoods and conservation against each other is a provincial decision — and today it is made largely blind.

2. The decisions that need data

The portal is built around the actual decisions people in and around Oro face. Four stand out.

Land use & agricultural expansion

Where should new oil palm or cocoa go, and where should it not? Answering this needs to know what is still forest, what is already cleared, what is protected, and what the terrain and soils allow — layered together for a specific place.

Conservation management of the Managalas Conservation Area

Managers and landowner committees need to know where forest is being cleared now, not in a report two years later, so limited ranger time can be targeted where it matters.

EUDR market access — time-bound and high-stakes

The EU Deforestation Regulation begins applying from 30 December 2026 (with later dates for small enterprises), and it reaches straight into Oro's economy: cocoa, coffee and palm oil are all in scope.[8] Buyers will require proof that a commodity did not come from land deforested after 2020, backed by the plot's geolocation — a point for plots up to 4 ha, a polygon for larger ones. PNG is rated low-risk, which simplifies due diligence, but the burden still falls hardest on smallholders, much of whose coffee and cocoa grows on plots that have never been formally surveyed; non-certified smallholders risk being shut out of the EU market altogether.[9] Being able to check a plot against a deforestation baseline, for free, before a buyer does, is now a direct livelihood question.

Climate, disaster and service planning

Fire risk rises sharply in El Niño years; active-fire and drought signals, and basic population and accessibility layers, support preparedness and the siting of services.

3. How the portal fills the gap

The gap is not that the data does not exist — much of it does, freely, from satellites and global programmes. The gap is that it is scattered, technical, stale in the places people look, and invisible to non-specialists. The portal is designed precisely against those four barriers.

The barrierWhat the portal does
ScatteredConsolidates satellite monitoring, land-cover and forest maps, protected-area boundaries and the region's own document archive into one place, framed on Oro Province.
StaleFeeds refresh automatically on a schedule — fire and clearing alerts daily, climate monthly — so the map reflects the latest data with no manual re-processing.
TechnicalA Site Analysis tool lets anyone draw or upload a plot and get back plain figures — area, forest cover, land cover, an EUDR deforestation screen — with no GIS software or skills.
InvisibleA friendly, low-bandwidth interface and a plain-language guide, usable on the slow, intermittent connections common in the province.

Crucially, it does this at very low cost. The portal is a lightweight access layer, not a data warehouse: it leans on free global datasets and free hosting, and the one piece of paid infrastructure — the on-demand analysis engine — is metered to run comfortably within a free tier at the province's expected usage. Keeping it running does not depend on a continuing grant for servers.

4. It is a proven model — brought to provincial scale

The idea that an open geospatial portal can change real decisions is not speculative; it is established at national scale and well evidenced. Through the World Resources Institute's Global Forest Watch and its national Forest Atlases, governments have used accessible forest data to extend and strengthen Indonesia's moratorium on new forest concessions, to calculate deforestation taxes in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and — in Cameroon — to let the forestry ministry make better-informed allocation decisions. Governments using these tools to design or implement policy now manage over 400 million hectares of forest; park rangers in Uganda use the same alerts to target patrols rather than searching at random.[10]

The Oro Data Portal applies this well-proven model where it has been missing: at the provincial scale, tuned to one province's crops, conservation area and decisions, in the hands of the people who actually manage the land — not a national ministry alone.

5. Worked examples

A cocoa cooperative prepares for the EUDR deadline
An officer uploads a member's garden boundary into Site Analysis. The tool returns the plot's area and an EUDR screen showing the share that was standing forest in 2020 and any cleared since — a self-check, for free, before an EU buyer's own due diligence, with time to act before December 2026.
A provincial land-use officer reviews a proposed oil-palm expansion
Before commenting on a development proposal, the officer opens the map over the site, switches on the 2020 forest benchmark and protected-area boundaries, and sees at a glance how much standing forest and how much of the Managalas Conservation Area the proposal would touch — in minutes, without a GIS unit.
Conservation managers target a ranger visit
A clearing-alert layer flags recent forest loss inside the conservation area. Managers focus a patrol on that location instead of covering ground at random — the same shift from random to targeted enforcement that rangers report elsewhere.
The programme reports on its own results
For annual reporting, staff pull the province's forest-loss trend and conservation-area status directly from the portal, turning monitoring that already runs into evidence of impact.

6. Alignment with the EU-FCCB programme

The portal is not a side activity; it is a direct instrument of the programme funding it. The EU Forestry, Climate Change and Biodiversity (EU-FCCB) Nexus Programme (2023–2027, €54.7 million) sets three strategic objectives, and the portal serves all three.[11]

EU-FCCB objectiveHow the portal contributes
1 · Evidence-based FCCB policy, governance & institutionsPuts current forest, land-cover and conservation data in front of provincial decision-makers — the literal meaning of evidence-based.
2 · Knowledge & awareness among the populationAn open, plain-language portal and guide make the state of the province's forests visible and understandable to communities, not just experts.
3 · Inclusive green investment & deforestation-free livelihoodsThe EUDR screening tool helps smallholders keep access to the EU market — the programme's deforestation-free-livelihoods goal, made concrete at plot level.

The programme's Managalas & Oro Province Component exists to enhance conservation while developing livelihoods around the Managalas Conservation Area;[12] the portal is the shared evidence base that both halves of that mission rely on.

7. Honest limitations

  • A screening tool, not a legal ruling. The EUDR check uses global satellite products to flag areas for a closer look; it is not an official or legal EUDR determination, and field and official verification remain necessary.
  • Satellite data has limits. Cloud, small clearings and mapping resolution mean figures are indicative; the portal is a guide to support judgement, not a replacement for it.
  • Connectivity. The portal is built for low bandwidth, but reaching the most remote users still depends on the province's improving connectivity.
  • It needs an owner. The portal's value grows when a provincial institution adopts it, keeps the local records current, and uses it in real processes. The technology is low-maintenance by design; the institutional home is what makes it last.

8. Conclusion

Oro Province is making consequential decisions about forests, farms and a nationally significant conservation area, largely without the data those decisions deserve — even though much of that data already exists, unused. The Oro Data Portal closes the distance between the data and the decision. It consolidates what is scattered, keeps it current, makes it legible to non-specialists, and does so cheaply and durably. The model is proven at national scale; the portal brings it to the province, and squarely serves the objectives of the programme that funds it. The gap is real, it is costly, and this is a practical way to fill it.
